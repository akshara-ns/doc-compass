SLIDE 2 — MOTIVATION: WHAT WE AUTOMATE AND WHY (Sohum, 1 min)

On it:   A three-step picture of the wrong path: book a specialist -> "you need a different doctor"
         -> start again (another copay, another waitlist).
         On the slide, not just said:

         Small text: specialties are split finely (podiatry vs orthopedics, optometry vs ophthalmology).

         (only add once counted; see the check list).

         directories list specialties but don't say which one fits your problem. It's worth
         automating because the decision is small, frequent and costly to get wrong, and the
         answer doesn't need a diagnosis.

SLIDE 4 — THE SYSTEM, AND WHAT WE ASSUMED (Akshara, 1 min 15 s)
Title:   Hand-written rules catch emergencies before any model runs
On it:   The system figure, simplified from docs/checkin/checkin_figure_v3.png, left to right,
         with a short label on every arrow:
           concern --raw text--> [1 red-flag rules] --no flag--> [2 scrub] --text-->
           [3 router] --top 3--> [4 explanation] --> sign shown to the user
           red arrow from 1 straight to "Seek emergency care now" (nothing else runs)
         One annotation per box:
           1  11 rules, each quoting a published warning sign (MedlinePlus / NIH, CDC)
           2  removes handles, emails, phone numbers, links
           3  three routers compared; best one ships
           4  off-the-shelf LLM; its text is checked, fixed wording is the fallback
         Assumptions box (bottom corner):
           - users describe their own concern, in English, without personal details
           - US outpatient care; "Start with a GP" is always a safe answer
           - emergencies are caught by rules, not by a model
Say:     The emergency check is deliberately not learned: a rule we can read and cite is safer
         than a model we have to trust. Only if no rule fires does the router run.


SLIDE 5 — DATA (Akshara, 1 min 10 s)
Title:   No public dataset fit the task, so we labelled real patient posts ourselves
On it:   Left: what exists and why it doesn't fit (3 short rows)
           clinician notes (MTSamples)        -> doctor's language, not a patient's
           synthetic triage sets               -> machine-written textbook cases
           Patient Comments (Mendeley)         -> one-line comments, not real posts;
                                                  we use it only for pre-training
         Right: our dataset
           600 real r/AskDocs posts (from 10,366 unique posts in MediQ_AskDocs)
           label = which doctor to book: 11 specialties + "Start with a GP"
           split by post: 400 train / 50 dev / 150 test; 60 test posts labelled by both of us
Say:     The label is a door, not a diagnosis, so it can follow a written guideline.
         Posts that aren't a "which doctor" question are skipped. Post text never leaves our laptops;
         we share ids and labels only.


SLIDE 6 — MODELS (Sohum, 1 min 10 s)
Title:   Two kinds of router tie on public data; an off-the-shelf LLM explains
On it:   Table (stage 1, same 938 held-out public comments):
           Model                              Type            Top-1     Top-3    Macro-F1
           TF-IDF + logistic regression       from scratch    94.0%     98.6%    0.902
           DistilRoBERTa                      fine-tuned      94.3%     99.0%    0.908
           BiomedBERT                         fine-tuned      94.2%     98.8%    0.904
         Small diagram: Stage 1: 6,252 public comments (938 of them held out for this table)
                        -> Stage 2: our 400 posts -> test on our 150
         Footnote: 95% intervals overlap (about ±1.6 points), so the three are tied on public data.
                   The public comments are short and alike, so ~94% is an easy score.
                   Our own 150 posts are the real test.
Say:     Stage 1 teaches the task on public data; stage 2 adapts to how real people write.
         One research question: does BiomedBERT, pretrained on biomedical research papers,
         beat a general encoder on patients' words? On public data: no difference.
         (BiomedBERT is also twice as deep, 12 layers to 6, so it is not a like-for-like test.)
         Off-the-shelf: Qwen2.5-1.5B writes the reason and the questions and is never trained.

SLIDE 7 — HOW ONE REQUEST WORKS (Akshara, 1 min)
Title:   Every answer is one of three signs, and an unsure router never pretends to be sure
On it:   Three screenshots side by side, each with one line under it:
           red     "Seek emergency care now"   a rule fired; nothing else ran
           blue    one specialty               confident: top choice is clear
           amber   two options, "you decide"   unsure: top confidence below a cutoff,
                                               or the top two close together
         Strip along the bottom: the LLM's reason must follow a fixed sentence, can't name a
         disease, drug or number, and can't name a doctor the router didn't pick; anything
         that fails is replaced by fixed wording.
         Small text: the cutoffs are placeholders until they are tuned on our dev posts.
Say:     A confident wrong answer is the worst outcome for a routing tool. So an unsure result
         shows two doors, and "Start with a GP" is always the fallback. The choice of doctor
         always comes from the router, never from the language model.

SLIDE 9 — REFLECTION AND NEXT STEPS (both, 1 min 10 s)
Title:   Public data got us running fast, and showed us exactly where it breaks
On it:   Small screenshot: "my gums bleed when I brush" -> Ob-Gyn, 94–96%
         Went well
           - rules-first design: the emergency path is readable and cited
           - a public set let the whole app work before our own labels existed
         Went poorly
           - the public set only knows "bleeding" as an Ob-Gyn word (the screenshot)
           - fine-tuned routers are over-confident (98–99% on easy inputs), so the two-option
             state rarely appears until the cutoffs are tuned
           - our first data plan (specialty subreddits) broke on Reddit's terms, and full
             redaction didn't fit the time, so we cut scope to routing
           - AI assistants sped up code and drafted the labels, which the authors reviewed
         Next
           - stage 2: train on our own posts, then report results on our own 150 test posts,
             against "always GP" and against the from-scratch router
           - tune the unsure cutoffs on dev
           - later: a human baseline (students picking a doctor for the same posts) and redaction
Say:     The gums example is our favourite failure: the model is fluent and wrong, which is
         why the test set is our own posts and not the public data.
Then:    "Questions?"
